Walk-up simple: make it easy to join the work

Use shared context, clear starting conditions, and a definition of done so people can find useful work quickly.

Imagine joining a team while its priorities are changing by the hour. Before you can help, you need to know what it is trying to do, what is ready to start, and what counts as finished.

“Walk-up simple” gives that problem a practical target: someone arriving at the work should understand how to contribute in about five minutes. For a startup with limited people and time, that is a useful standard for how to share context.

#Put the information where the work happens

The Japanese word gemba refers to the place where work creates value. That can be a factory floor, an operating room, a building site, a kitchen, or a programmer's workstation. For a small team, it may be an office or a shared Slack channel.

A gemba walk takes leaders to that place so they can observe the work, find waste, and identify improvements. It has something in common with management by walking around (MBWA): understanding the work requires contact with the people doing it.

The same principle applies to information. Put goals, priorities, and progress together where the team can see them. A new colleague or an outside partner should be able to find the context they need to start helping.

#Make the board answer useful questions

A shared board can show requests coming in, work in progress, and completed work. Two definitions help make it readable: the definition of ready (DoR) explains when work can start; the definition of done (DoD) explains when it is complete.

Write completion criteria in concrete terms: “We are done when this happens.” Someone arriving at the board should be able to identify useful work within roughly five to seven minutes.

#Give people room to move

The Law of Two Feet asks you to consider whether your presence is useful where you are. If you can contribute or learn, stay. If another place needs you more, move there.

This depends on accessible context. People cannot switch roles or help with a new problem quickly if every move requires a long briefing. The idea can be expressed at a dramatic scale, such as bringing 10,000 people toward a problem within minutes. I treat that as an ambition for coordination, not a measured result or a reason to skip preparation.

#Keep it useful as the team grows

Clear, shared information helps new people join the work sooner. It reduces misunderstandings and duplicate effort, gives growing teams a common reference, and makes it easier to share ideas.

The test is simple enough to try at your own board: can someone who just arrived explain the goal, choose a useful task, and tell when that task is finished? If they need you to narrate everything, some of the context is still missing.
